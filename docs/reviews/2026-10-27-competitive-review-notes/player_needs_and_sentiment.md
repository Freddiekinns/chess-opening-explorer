# Player needs and sentiment: how 800–1800 adult improvers feel about learning openings and opening tools (2024–2026)

> Method note. These notes rely on the WebSearch tool alone. Reddit is
> **blocked** for the search tool (`allowed_domains: reddit.com` returned "not
> accessible to our user agent"), so Reddit voice appears only where it is
> quoted on other sites. App Store and Play Store reviews were reached through
> aggregators (JustUseApp, AppFollow, mwm.ai, marlvel.ai), not the stores
> themselves. Quotes are as returned in search snippets; they are short
> paraphrases or fragments, not verified full posts. Frequency ratings (High /
> Medium / Low) are my judgement from how many independent sources raised a
> theme in about 20 searches. They are not counts.

## What do forums, blogs and app reviews say about opening tools (Chessable, Chessbook, Lichess, Chess.com, Chessly, YouTube, AI coaches)?

### Takeaway

The loudest and most consistent complaint is about **relevance, not quantity**.
Improvers say they spend hours drilling lines they never meet at their level.
Chessable is criticised for that, for price and for app bugs, but people still
respect its explained courses. Chessbook gets praise for "only the moves you'll
actually face". Lichess users like its free explorer and keep asking for a
built-in trainer. Chess.com users resent how little of the explorer is free.
Chessly is liked for short videos and quizzes. Users don't trust raw chatbot
explanations of openings.

  [Lichess blog, Busbar](https://lichess.org/@/Busbar/blog/can-ai-help-me-us-build-better-chess-training-part-1/TtEM87xh)

  evidence and the LLM supplies the prose. —
  [RV Chess ChatGPT app](https://chatgpt.com/plugins/plugin_asdk_app_6ac04ce1b5448191837e6fea806c0084)

### Inferences

- The improver's core complaint across tools is **"I am drilling the wrong
  things"**. Chessbook's popularity comes from addressing that by putting
  frequency at the user's level first.
- The second complaint is **"I don't know why"**. Chessable's authored prose and
  Chessly's plan quizzes are what fans defend. A tool that combines frequency at
  the user's level with short "why" explanations sits in the gap between the
  two.
- Chatbot explanations of openings carry a trust deficit among engaged players.
  Any AI explanation should be visibly grounded in engine and database evidence.

### Gaps

- No direct Reddit threads could be read; Reddit is blocked for the search tool.
  No first-hand Play Store review text was found.
- I found no reliable figures on churn or "review backlog abandonment" for
  Chessable or Chessbook.
- Chessbook's current free cap is unresolved: 200 per its site versus 400 per
  its App Store listing.
- The Chess.com "four moves free" claim comes from a student op-ed and is
  unverified.

## What are the jobs players are trying to get done, and how strong is each?

### Takeaway

Six recurring jobs stand out:

1. Learn only what I'll actually face (strongest).
2. Remember my lines.
3. Understand the plans when opponents leave theory early.
4. Pick an opening that suits me.
5. Prepare for a specific club or league opponent.
6. Know how much theory is enough at my rating.

### Cited Findings

- **"Opponents leave theory at move 3–10" (High).**
  - A Zwischenzug sample of eight Lichess blitz games at about 2000 left
    mainstream theory between moves 3 and 10, averaging just under six theory
    moves. The sample is small and from a higher band than 800–1800.
  - Forum commenters conclude that deep memorisation has limited value at that
    level. —
    [Lichess forum, "which Sicilian gives the least theory"](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/white-player-vs-sicilian-which-sicilian-gives-the-least-theory-to-learn-please?page=3)
- **"What will I face at my level?" (High).**
  - Advice is to set the Lichess database rating and time-control filters to
    your own pool, and to "focus on the moves that are most common at your
    level, not necessarily the 'book' moves". —
    [Zwischenzug, "How to use the opening explorer"](https://zwischenzug.substack.com/p/how-to-use-the-opening-explorer)
  - Chessbook's adoption story is the same job. —
    [mattplayschess](https://mattplayschess.substack.com/p/chessbook-a-game-changer-for-adult)
- **"I forget my lines" (High).** The advice converges on a few habits:
  - spaced repetition, which "only works if you actually do it"
  - writing your prep from memory into a blank study against a timer
  - understanding the why so you can find a forgotten move over the board
  - playing the lines in games
  - shrinking the repertoire —
    [Zwischenzug, "How to stop forgetting your openings"](https://zwischenzug.substack.com/p/how-to-stop-forgetting-your-openings);
    [Zwischenzug, "How to remember your openings"](https://zwischenzug.substack.com/p/how-to-remember-your-openings);
    [Chess.com forum](https://chess.com/forum/view/for-beginners/how-do-people-remember-all-chess-moves-in-openings);
    [Chess.com blog, EKislik](https://chess.com/blog/EKislik/what-to-do-when-you-forget-your-openings)
  - A forum view: "if you vary your plays TOO much then you're not really gonna
    retain anything". —
    [Chess.com forum](https://chess.com/forum/view/for-beginners/how-do-people-remember-all-chess-moves-in-openings)
  - ChessAtlas markets "flags where your real games left your prep", a product
    built directly on this job. —
    [ChessAtlas listing](https://mwm.ai/apps/chess-openings-chessatlas/6770867404)
- **"Which opening suits me?" (Medium).**
  - Chess.com's personality quiz suggests openings. —
    [Chess.com news](https://www.chess.com/news/view/chess-personality?page=4)
  - The Chessiverse style quiz maps sharp players to the King's Gambit and
    Sicilian, and positional players to the Caro-Kann and Slav. It warns that a
    mismatched style "can cost you games". —
    [Chessiverse quiz](https://chessiverse.com/chess-style-quiz);
    [Chessiverse guide](https://chessiverse.com/blog/chess-personality-and-opening-choice)
  - Silman's "choose openings that suit your style/temperament" is still quoted.
    — [Lichess blog](https://lichess.org/ublog/sA7Vyofp/redirect)
  - Heisman (2001) says the same. —
    [Chess.com forum](https://chess.com/forum/view/chess-openings/in-need-of-chess-books-for-chess-openings)
- **"Prepare for a club or league opponent" (Low–Medium; niche but intense).**
  - Common workflow: the Lichess explorer Player tab, filtered by time control
    and date. —
    [CheckRaiseMate blog](https://lichess.org/@/CheckRaiseMate/blog/how-to-use-the-opening-explorer/H4PMsjTh)
  - Chessboard Magic's Opponent Prep compares two Lichess or Chess.com profiles
    with an "Opening SWOT". —
    [HollowLeaf blog](https://lichess.org/@/HollowLeaf/blog/opponent-prep-updated/uk1kdQWg)
  - Players warn that sample sizes are small, and spend "10–15 minutes most
    weeks" on prep. —
    [Lichess blog, pulsar512b](https://en.lichess.org/@/pulsar512b/blog/prep-a-discussion/5C1Uu2Ze)
  - OpeningTree, once the favourite, is listed as discontinued. —
    [AlternativeTo](https://alternativeto.net/software/openingtree/about)
- **"Practise against realistic replies" (Medium; unmet).**
  - Lichess users ask for a bot that plays the opening and varies
    sub-variations. —
    [Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/lichess-feedback/learning-openings-with-bots-?page=1)
  - Chessly's Levy bot is praised for exactly this. —
    [Chess.com forum](https://chess.com/forum/view/general/gotham-chess-new-chessly-course?page=4)
  - Chessreps markets drills built from real Lichess games. —
    [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6744000612)
- **"Free repertoire tool" (Medium).** Recurring forum threads ask for one.
  Answers rotate through:
  - ChessTempo
  - Chess Repertoire Companion ("free… no ads… mobile friendly")
  - Chessable custom courses
  - Listudy
  - LucasChess
  - Chessdriller Chess Position Trainer is reported unsupported. —
    [Chess.com forum, "Chessable alternatives?"](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/general/chessable-alternatives);
    [Chess.com forum, "Opening repertoire trainer?"](https://chess.com/forum/view/chess-openings/opening-repertoire-trainer);
    [Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/free-open-source-pgn-trainer-software-with-spaced-repetition)

### Inferences

- "Learn what I'll face" and "remember it" are two halves of one loop:
  1. See what is common at my rating.
  2. Pick a small set of replies.
  3. Drill them.
  4. Check against my real games where I deviated.

  No free tool owns the whole loop. Chessbook comes closest and is paid beyond
  its cap. Lichess has steps 1 and 4 (explorer, Player tab) but no step 3.

- Preparing for an opponent is a smaller audience, but it matches club and
  league players exactly. It also leans on free Lichess data that a third party
  can surface.

### Gaps

- I have no quantitative ranking of these jobs. Frequency is inferred from how
  often sources recur.
- I found no data specific to 800–1500 on the move at which opponents leave
  theory; the only figure is from about 2000 blitz.

## What do players say about YouTube versus drilling versus playing?

### Takeaway

Players treat YouTube and Chessly-style video as the low-friction way in, liked
for ideas and personality, and drilling as the way to retain lines. Experienced
voices say playing the opening in real games is what makes it stick. The pattern
most recommend is a mix: watch for ideas, drill a small tree, play it, then
review the deviations.

### Cited Findings

- Sceptics of paid courses recommend free YouTube plus tactics, because most
  course lines never appear in their games. —
  [Chess.com forum](https://chess.com/forum/view/general/gotham-chess-new-chessly-course?page=4)
- Chessly fans value short videos and plan-based quizzes over rote moves. —
  [r/GothamChess mirror](https://lr.in.psf.lt/r/GothamChess)
- Instead of relying on Chessable review, test openings in real games. —
  [datajunkie Lichess blog](https://lichess.org/@/datajunkie/blog/4-lifetime-repertoires-10-things-to-avoid-when-learning-openings-on-chessable/SedowvEI)
- Lines stick once they have worked in a real game; playing many rated games is
  described as natural spaced exposure. —
  [Chess.com forum](https://chess.com/forum/view/for-beginners/how-do-people-remember-all-chess-moves-in-openings);
  [Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/lichess-feedback/learning-openings-with-bots-?page=1)
- Model games teach typical piece placement and plans for both sides. Tamburro's
  _Openings for Amateurs_ is pitched at club players who "can't afford to learn
  variations 30 moves deep" and uses 53 annotated model games. —
  [Lichess blog, datajunkie "Studying chess openings: 10 tips"](https://lichess.org/@/datajunkie/blog/studying-chess-openings-10-tips/Mulq2TCS);
  [Bokus listing](https://www.bokus.com/bok/9781936277575/openings-for-amateurs)

### Inferences

- An explorer that links each position to short videos (ideas), its frequency at
  the player's level (relevance) and a light drill (retention) covers all three
  study modes. Each competitor covers one or two.

### Gaps

- I found no survey quantifying how improvers split time between YouTube,
  drilling and playing.

## What is the prevailing coaching advice on openings for sub-1800 players, and how should a tool respect it?

### Takeaway

The consensus:

- Tactics come first for anyone still hanging pieces.
- Principles plus a **small** repertoire, understood rather than memorised, from
  about 900–1000 upwards (Naroditsky as reported).
- Avoid memorising long lines (Heisman).
- Learn the common traps, mainly so you can avoid them.
- Choose by temperament.

A tool that respects this keeps repertoires small, shows the why, weights by
frequency at the player's level and doesn't push depth.

### Cited Findings

- Naroditsky, reported second-hand from his "Back to 3000" series: start
  openings at about 900–1000; have a repertoire, memorise some main lines **and
  understand them**. Tactics take priority while a player still hangs pieces. —
  [Listudy blog, "When to start learning chess openings?"](https://listudy.org/en/blog/when-to-learn-chess-openings)
- Heisman (2005, quoted): not a fan of weaker players memorising many lines
  they'll never play, but a little time improving how they play their openings
  is worthwhile. —
  [Lichess forum](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/how-should-i-study-chess-openings)
  - Heisman (2007): read annotated games, which teach all phases together, and
    avoid repeated traps. —
    [Chess.com forum](https://chess.com/forum/view/chess-openings/how-do-we-study-openings)
  - Heisman conjectured that memorising MCO-15 would change ratings very little.
    —
    [Chess.com blog, Kingpatzer](https://www.chess.com/blog/Kingpatzer/yes-study-openings)
- "Save intense memorisation until about 1800" comes from an **anonymous forum
  commenter**, not Heisman. —
  [Chess.com forum](https://chess.com/forum/view/chess-openings/how-do-we-study-openings)
- Counter-views:
  - Repeated warnings against memorisation can confuse real beginners. —
    [US Chess forum](https://forum.uschess.org/t/what-openings-to-teach/173?page=2)
  - "a memorised principle is just as bad as a memorised opening". —
    [Lichess blog discussion, "Why beginners shouldn't study openings"](https://lichess.org/ublog/8lDyZijK/discuss)
- Traps:
  - Useful short term in blitz and bullet but of little long-term value. —
    [Lichess forum post](https://www.lichess.org/forum/redirect/post/adn3GXEa)
  - Knowing traps also stops opponents using them on you. —
    [ChessBase, 2020](https://en.chessbase.com/post/learn-the-best-opening-traps)
  - The ideas behind traps carry over to the middlegame. —
    [ChessClub.com](https://www.chessclub.com/article/tricks-and-traps-everywhere)
- Simplify the repertoire and cut sidelines if you keep forgetting. —
  [Obsidian Chess Studio guide](https://www.mintlify.com/luisrivasnoriega/Obsidian-Chess-Studio/guide/opening-repertoire)

### Inferences

- A tool aligned with coaching consensus would:
  - default to rating-banded stats (Lichess 1000–1800), not masters
  - suggest one main reply for White and one for Black against each common
    opponent move
  - mark "traps to know (to avoid)" rather than "traps to win with"
  - pair each line with its plan and model games
  - avoid gamifying depth
- Copy matters. "You don't need more than this at your level" is a feature
  players would welcome, given how much they complain about bloat.

### Gaps

- I found no primary transcript of Naroditsky's advice; the attribution is
  second-hand via Listudy, a vendor blog.
- I found no Hanging Pawns or Chessable-blog coaching piece from 2025–26 in
  these searches.

## Which needs are repeatedly mentioned but unmet?

### Takeaway

The recurring unmet needs:

1. Free spaced repetition on Lichess, or any free polished trainer.
2. Lines weighted by how often they occur at my level.
3. Practice against realistic, varied opponent replies.
4. A "why" for each move, from a source users trust.
5. A link back from my own games showing where I deviated.
6. Easy opponent prep now that OpeningTree is gone.
7. Better mobile trainer UX: filtered review, no lag.

### Cited Findings

- Spaced repetition on Lichess has been requested repeatedly, with no official
  answer. —

### Inferences

- For a free, SEO-led explorer the most defensible openings are:
  - "common at my level" views per position (rating-banded frequency plus a
    plain-English plan)
  - "what to play against X" answers (one recommended reply, with alternatives)
  - links to trusted video explanations

  Each answers a high-frequency job without competing head-on with Chessable or
  Chessbook trainers.

- A light, free drill of a small tree would meet the Lichess request. It would
  be a larger build, and there is a crowded field of small tools (Chessdriller,
  ChessTempo, Listudy, Chessreps, ChessAtlas, Hard Lines), none of which has
  broken through.

### Gaps

- I couldn't quantify demand for each unmet need; I found no surveys or vote
  counts.
- Reddit, the largest venue for this conversation, could not be searched
  directly, so its frequency and tone are under-represented here.
